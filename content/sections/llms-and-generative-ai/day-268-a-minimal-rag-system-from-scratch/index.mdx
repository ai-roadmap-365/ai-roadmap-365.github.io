## Why this matters

You have used retrieval for four days without ever seeing it work. Yesterday's
lesson gave you the architecture; today you build the whole thing in about a
hundred lines of pure Python, with no libraries at all.

That is not a stunt. **You cannot debug what you have only ever imported.** When
a production pipeline returns a plausible answer sourced from the wrong
document, the question is always the same — did retrieval fail, did fusion fail,
or did the prompt fail? Someone who has written those three stages can answer in
minutes. Someone who has only configured them is left restarting the vector
database and hoping.

The whole apparatus reduces to arithmetic you already know:

```text
  chunk        split text on word boundaries with an overlap
  index        count terms (BM25) and store vectors (embeddings)
  retrieve     score every chunk, keep the top k
  fuse         combine two rankings by position, not by score
  assemble     paste the survivors into a prompt with numbered labels
```

There is no step in that list that requires a framework. There is no step that
cannot be tested. And once you have written it, every later decision — which
vector store, which reranker, which chunk size — becomes a comparison against
something you understand rather than a guess between two brand names.

## The idea in plain language

A minimal RAG system is **four small functions in a row**, and the only
interesting mathematics is the dot product.

Ask a librarian for "the paper about attention in translation". They do two
things at once without noticing. They look for those literal words on spines and
in indexes — that is lexical search, and it is what BM25 does. They also think
about what you *meant*, and fetch a paper titled "Neural Machine Translation by
Jointly Learning to Align and Translate" that contains none of your words — that
is semantic search, and it is what an embedding does.

Neither is sufficient. Lexical search fails on synonyms; semantic search fails
on exact identifiers, error codes and part numbers. So you run both and merge
the two lists. Then you hand the survivors to someone who can write, with the
instruction: use only these pages, and cite them.

That is the entire system. Today you write it.

## Historical background

The two halves of retrieval were invented decades apart and have only recently
been used together.

- **1972** — Karen Spärck Jones publishes inverse document frequency: a term
  that appears everywhere tells you nothing, so weight it down. Every lexical
  search engine since rests on that one idea.
- **1994** — Robertson and Walker formalise **BM25** at TREC-3. It is still a
  strong baseline in 2026, which is remarkable for a thirty-year-old ranking
  function with two tunable constants.
- **2013** — word2vec makes dense vectors cheap, and "similar meaning" becomes
  "small angle between two arrows".
- **2019** — Sentence-BERT produces embeddings where cosine similarity between
  *sentences* is meaningful, not just between words.
- **2020** — Lewis et al. name **Retrieval-Augmented Generation** and Karpukhin
  et al. publish Dense Passage Retrieval, showing dense retrieval beating BM25
  on open-domain question answering.
- **2009** — out of sequence, and worth it: Cormack et al. describe
  **Reciprocal Rank Fusion**, a method for merging rankings that needs no score
  calibration at all. It was designed for meta-search engines. It turned out to
  be exactly what hybrid retrieval needed a decade later.

The lesson of that timeline is that nothing here is new or proprietary. The
pieces are old, well-documented, and small enough to write yourself.

## What it is — and what it is not

**It is** a complete, honest, end-to-end RAG pipeline. Every stage a production
system has, this has: chunking, lexical indexing, dense indexing, fusion, prompt
assembly. The results are real results, and the failures are real failures.

**It is not** production infrastructure, and pretending otherwise would teach
you the wrong lesson. Specifically it lacks:

| Missing | Why it does not matter today | When it starts to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Persistence | The index rebuilds in under a second | A corpus that takes minutes to embed |
| Approximate nearest neighbour | An exhaustive scan of 10,000 vectors is fast | Roughly 100,000 chunks and up |
| Concurrency | One query at a time is fine while learning | More than one user |
| A real embedding model | A hashed bag-of-words shows the mechanics | Any question about actual quality |

The last row deserves emphasis. Today's "embeddings" are deterministic hashes,
not learned representations. They demonstrate the *plumbing* of dense retrieval
faithfully while telling you nothing about its *quality*. That distinction —
between a mechanism that runs and a mechanism that works — is the one this
course keeps returning to.

## Why it was created and what problems it solves

Frameworks hide the seams, and the seams are where the bugs live.

Ask a working RAG team what actually went wrong in their last incident and you
will rarely hear "the vector database was down". You will hear things like these,
every one of which lives in a seam between two stages:

- The chunker split a table across two chunks, so no chunk contained both the
  column heading and the number underneath it.
- The retriever returned excellent chunks and the prompt template truncated them
  at 2,000 characters, silently, from the end.
- Two documents were near-duplicates, so the top three results were three copies
  of the same passage and the answer had one source wearing three hats.
- The embedding of the query was computed with a different model version from
  the embeddings in the index, and every score was quietly meaningless.

None of those is visible from a configuration file. All of them are obvious to
someone who has written the four functions and knows what each hands to the
next. Building it once is the cheapest insurance available against a whole
category of bug.

## How it works

![Minimal In-Memory RAG Pipeline diagram](./assets/minimal-rag-in-memory-pipeline.svg)

### 1. Chunking on word boundaries, with overlap

Text is split into windows of `chunk_size` words that overlap by `overlap`
words. The overlap exists because a fact can straddle a boundary: a sentence
ending "...the write cache costs" followed by "$3.75 per million tokens" is
useless if the two halves land in different chunks and only one is retrieved.

The loop must terminate on `i + chunk_size >= len(words)`, or the final partial
window is emitted twice — a bug the lab's tests catch deliberately.

### 2. BM25, in about fifteen lines

For a query term *q* and document *d*, BM25 scores

```text
  IDF(q) · ( f(q,d) · (k1 + 1) ) / ( f(q,d) + k1 · (1 - b + b · |d|/avgdl) )
```

`f(q,d)` is the term's frequency in the document. `k1` (typically 1.5) saturates
that frequency, so the twentieth occurrence of a word adds far less than the
second. `b` (typically 0.75) normalises for document length, so a long document
does not win simply by containing more words. `IDF` down-weights terms that
appear everywhere.

You do not need to memorise the formula. You do need to know that it produces
**unbounded positive scores** — which is why the next stage cannot simply add it
to a cosine similarity.

### 3. Dense retrieval as a dot product

Every chunk becomes a vector. Every query becomes a vector. Relevance is the
cosine of the angle between them, and for unit-normalised vectors cosine
similarity *is* the dot product — one multiply-add per dimension, nothing more.

Scores land in `[-1, 1]`. BM25's do not. That mismatch is the whole reason the
next stage exists.

### 4. Reciprocal Rank Fusion

RRF throws the scores away and keeps only the positions:

```text
  RRF(d) = Σ  1 / (k + rank_i(d))          k = 60 by convention
```

A document ranked first in either list contributes `1/61`. Ranked tenth,
`1/70`. Because only ranks enter the sum, BM25's unbounded scale and cosine's
bounded one never have to be reconciled — there is nothing to normalise and no
weight to tune. A document that both methods rank highly rises above one that
either ranks first alone, which is exactly the behaviour you want.

### 5. Prompt assembly

The survivors are numbered and pasted into the context with an instruction to
answer only from them and to cite by number. This stage looks trivial and is
where a surprising share of production failures happen: silent truncation,
missing separators between chunks, and citation labels that do not match the
order the chunks were pasted in.

## Examples in practice

The four stages, at the size they actually are.

**Chunking**

```python
def chunk(text: str, size: int = 120, overlap: int = 20) -> list[str]:
    words, out, i = text.split(), [], 0
    while i < len(words):
        out.append(" ".join(words[i : i + size]))
        if i + size >= len(words):      # last window; stop or it repeats
            break
        i += size - overlap
    return out
```

**Lexical scoring**

```python
def bm25(query: str, docs: list[list[str]], k1=1.5, b=0.75) -> list[float]:
    N, avgdl = len(docs), sum(len(d) for d in docs) / len(docs)
    terms = re.findall(r"\w+", query.lower())      # lowercase, or nothing matches
    scores = [0.0] * N
    for t in terms:
        n_t = sum(1 for d in docs if t in d)
        if not n_t:
            continue
        idf = math.log(1 + (N - n_t + 0.5) / (n_t + 0.5))
        for i, d in enumerate(docs):
            f = d.count(t)
            scores[i] += idf * (f * (k1 + 1)) / (f + k1 * (1 - b + b * len(d) / avgdl))
    return scores
```

**Fusion**

```python
def rrf(*rankings: list[int], k: int = 60) -> list[int]:
    score: dict[int, float] = {}
    for ranking in rankings:
        for rank, doc_id in enumerate(ranking, start=1):
            score[doc_id] = score.get(doc_id, 0.0) + 1.0 / (k + rank)
    return sorted(score, key=score.get, reverse=True)
```

![Naive RAG Prompt Synthesis Flow diagram](./assets/naive-rag-prompt-synthesis-flow.svg)

**Prompt assembly**

```python
def build_prompt(question: str, chunks: list[str]) -> str:
    context = "\n\n".join(f"[{i}] {c}" for i, c in enumerate(chunks, start=1))
    return (
        "Answer using ONLY the context below. Cite sources as [1], [2].\n"
        "If the context does not contain the answer, say so.\n\n"
        f"{context}\n\nQuestion: {question}"
    )
```

That is the system. Roughly sixty lines, four tests, no dependencies.

## Implications: security, privacy, performance, scalability, and cost

**Performance.** Scoring is a linear scan: every query touches every chunk. At
10,000 chunks of 120 words that is milliseconds and entirely acceptable. The
scan is what an approximate index later replaces, and Day 326 measures exactly
what that trade buys and costs.

**Scalability.** Memory holds the whole corpus twice — once as text, once as
vectors. A float32 vector of 384 dimensions is 1.5 KB, so 100,000 chunks is
about 150 MB of vectors before the text. That is the point at which "keep it in
a Python list" stops being a reasonable answer.

**Security.** Retrieval is an access-control boundary, and an in-memory list has
none. Anything in the index is reachable by any query. In production the filter
must be applied *before* chunks enter the context, not after — a model that has
seen a passage cannot be asked to forget it.

**Privacy.** Chunks are copies. A document deleted at source remains in the
index, and in the embeddings, until something removes it — which is why Day 325
treats freshness as a correctness property rather than a nicety.

**Cost.** Zero today, and that is itself instructive: every cost in a production
RAG system comes from the embedding model, the vector store, or the generation
call. The retrieval logic is free.

## Alternatives: free, open source, and commercial

| Option | Licence | What it gives you | What it costs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Writing it yourself | — | Complete understanding; no dependency risk | You maintain it |
| LangChain | MIT, free | Chains, loaders, and a large integration surface | Abstraction depth; upgrades can move behaviour |
| LlamaIndex | MIT, free | Indexing and query engines aimed squarely at RAG | Opinionated structure |
| Haystack | Apache-2.0, free | Explicit, typed pipeline graphs | More ceremony for small jobs |
| `rank_bm25` | Apache-2.0, free | Just the lexical half, well tested | Nothing else |

The honest recommendation is neither "always build" nor "always import". Build it
once, today, so you can read a framework's source and recognise what it is doing.
Then import, because a maintained BM25 implementation has fixed edge cases yours
has not met yet.

## Comparison with related concepts

| Approach | Where knowledge lives | Update cost | Attribution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| This pipeline | An external index | Re-index one document | Exact chunk, by number |
| Fine-tuning | Model weights | A full training run | None |
| Long-context stuffing | The prompt itself | Free | Whole document only |
| Plain keyword search | An inverted index | Re-index | Exact, but no synthesis |

Against **fine-tuning**: retrieval changes what the model *knows*; fine-tuning
changes how it *behaves*. Day 274 draws that line properly.

Against **long-context stuffing**: if the corpus fits in the context window,
stuffing is simpler and often better. Retrieval earns its complexity when the
corpus does not fit, or when you need to say which passage an answer came from.

## When to use it — and when not to

**Use a hand-written pipeline when** you are learning the mechanism, the corpus
is small enough to scan exhaustively, you need zero dependencies, or you are
building a reference implementation to test a framework against.

**Reach for a library when** the corpus outgrows memory, you need persistence
across restarts, several people query at once, or you need real embeddings from
a maintained model.

**Do not use retrieval at all when** the answer is already in the model, the
corpus fits comfortably in the context window, or the question is about
reasoning rather than facts. Retrieval adds two failure modes — retrieving the
wrong thing, and retrieving nothing — and they are only worth accepting when
grounding is genuinely required.

### Troubleshooting

- **Issue:** BM25 returns zero relevance scores for exact keyword searches.
  - **Fix:** Ensure input query terms are tokenized and lowercased using `re.findall(r'\w+', query.lower())`.
- **Issue:** Document chunker creates duplicate final chunks.
  - **Fix:** Ensure sliding window loop terminates when `i + chunk_size >= len(words)`.

### Common mistakes

- **Naive String Slicing:** Slicing raw strings every 1000 characters cuts words in half. Always chunk across token or whitespace boundaries.
- **Ignoring Document Frequency in BM25:** Forgetting to compute corpus-wide IDF makes common stopwords dominate search rankings.
