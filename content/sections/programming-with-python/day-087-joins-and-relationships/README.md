# Day 087 — Joins and Relationships

The third day of Week 13 ("SQL and Relational Databases"). Day 85 gave the
relational model and SQLite; Day 86 gave `SELECT` with filtering, sorting,
grouping and aggregates, including NULL's three-valued logic. Both worked inside
one table at a time. Today answers the question those two days deferred: why is
the data in more than one table at all?

The through-line: splitting a table fixes three specific failures and creates
one new need — putting the pieces back together on demand. That is a join.

The lesson opens from the mistake rather than the rule. One wide table with the
author's details repeated on every book row, and then the three anomalies
committed against it concretely: rename an author and miss a row, so the
database contradicts itself with no error raised; try to record an author whose
books have not been catalogued and find there is no row shape that holds one;
withdraw a book and lose the only record that its author existed. It then covers
primary and foreign keys and what a foreign key actually promises (not an index,
not the join itself); one-to-many with the key on the many side; many-to-many
and the junction table keyed on the pair; `INNER JOIN` with an explicit `ON`
against the older comma-join-with-`WHERE` form; `LEFT OUTER JOIN` and exactly
which rows survive and which columns come back NULL; the `LEFT JOIN` plus
`IS NULL` anti-join idiom; `CROSS JOIN` and the accidental cartesian product;
self-joins; joins across three and four tables; joins with `GROUP BY` and the
zero-count trap; `ON` against `WHERE` on an outer join; and the SQLite fact that
foreign-key enforcement is off by default and must be enabled per connection.

Two things in the lesson are proved rather than asserted, both from real runs on
the authoring machine. The foreign-key pragma: an orphan row is inserted
successfully, then the identical statement is rejected once the pragma is on,
in the shell and again in Python — including the documented trap that makes the
pragma a silent no-op inside an open transaction, which is why it appears to do
nothing when issued after a first `INSERT` in Python. And the `ON` against
`WHERE` distinction, where moving one predicate is wrong in both directions at
once: it drops a member who has genuinely returned everything and keeps a member
who has never borrowed at all.

The from-scratch requirement (A14) is met by implementing a nested-loop join and
a hash join in plain Python over lists of dictionaries, comparing their cost as
a product against a sum, and checking both against SQLite for exact equality —
the machinery the query planner is choosing between, made concrete.

The AI connection: joins are how a retrieval system ties an embedding back to
its source document, its version, its permissions and its freshness date. The
chunk you retrieve is useless without the row it came from, and a missing
permissions join is a query that runs perfectly while returning data to the
wrong person.
