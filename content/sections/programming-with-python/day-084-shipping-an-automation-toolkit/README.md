# Day 084 — Shipping an Automation Toolkit

The capstone lesson of Week 12 ("Python for Automation and the Web"). Days 78 to
83 introduced the pieces one at a time — HTTP with `requests`, responsible
collection, argparse CLIs, scheduling and background jobs, a FastAPI service,
packaging and console entry points. Today assembles them into one installable,
scheduled, tested tool, and teaches the judgement about what belongs in an
automation and what does not.

The through-line: an automation is not a script you ran once that worked. It is
something that runs unattended, on someone else's machine, when you are asleep,
and the difference is almost entirely in the parts that are not the happy path.

The lesson covers what shipping means for a personal tool (installable,
configurable, observable, safe to run twice); the architecture of a toolkit —
one package, several commands, a shared pure core, and every boundary pushed to
the edges as Day 74 argued; configuration precedence across defaults, file,
environment and flags, with provenance recorded so the tool can explain itself;
secrets from the environment only, redaction on the way out, and the correct
order of operations when a token leaks; structured logging as the thing that
makes an unattended run debuggable; failure design — what to retry, what to skip
and report, what to stop for — and partial success as the normal case with its
own exit code; idempotence, the state file, and the atomic write that keeps it
trustworthy; dry run as a first-class feature; observability for a personal tool,
including a watchdog that alerts on silence rather than only on error; and
packaging with console entry points and a real schedule entry.

The last third is judgement rather than mechanism: the honest arithmetic of
whether to automate at all, the automation that costs more than the manual task,
the automation nobody understands after six months, writing the runbook, and
knowing when to delete it.

The AI connection closes the week: this is the shape of every data and model
pipeline in the rest of the course — fetch, validate, transform, store, report,
on a schedule, with retries and partial-failure handling — and the operational
habits here are what separate a notebook that worked once from a pipeline that
runs for a year.

## How this lesson is rendered

`index.mdx` provides the lesson body, and the sidecar YAML files travel with it:
the site layout renders the body at the day route, injects the quiz from
`quiz.yml`, the glossary from `glossary.yml`, and the source list from
`sources.yml`, and uses `lesson.yml` for navigation, metadata, and time
estimates. Images are referenced from `index.mdx` with relative paths into
`assets/`, and their alt text must stay in sync with `visuals.yml`. All
cross-links (to the lab, to neighboring days) are generated by the layout from
central configuration — never hard-code repository or site URLs inside content
files.
