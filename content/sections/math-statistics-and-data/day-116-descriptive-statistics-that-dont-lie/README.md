# Day 116 — Descriptive Statistics That Don’t Lie

The fourth lesson of Week 17 ("Probability and Statistics"), following
probability rules, random variables and Bayes' theorem. Week 17 ends in an
A/B Test Analyzer on Day 119; today builds the descriptive vocabulary Day
117's sampling theory and Day 118's hypothesis tests both depend on.

The organising sentence, returned to throughout:

> **A summary statistic is a compression, and every compression discards.
> The only professional question is: what did this one hide?**

## The opening failure

The lesson opens with Anscombe's quartet: four datasets published by
Francis Anscombe in 1973 that agree, to documented precision, on mean x,
mean y, variance x, variance y, correlation, and regression slope — and
look nothing alike in shape. One is a roughly linear scatter, one is a
perfect parabola, one is a perfect line dragged by a single outlier, and
one has a single non-repeated x-value that alone determines the fitted
slope. Every familiar summary statistic agrees across all four. The lesson
verifies that agreement directly, then computes three further diagnostics
— none of them part of Anscombe's original table — that finally separate
the four shapes, which is the entire lesson of the lesson, demonstrated
rather than stated.

## What the lesson covers, each with a worked number

- Mean, median and mode from scratch, and exactly when each misleads,
  including `statistics.mode()`'s silent tie-breaking.
- The breakdown point: a corrupted salary list where one value drags the
  mean by over $1,104,444 and does not move the median at all.
- Bessel's correction, measured by 20,000-trial simulation rather than
  asserted: dividing by n underestimates the true variance by a measured
  factor of 0.8007, matching the predicted `(5-1)/5 = 0.8000`.
- Percentile ambiguity: the same eight numbers' 75th percentile computed
  under nine of NumPy's documented `method=` conventions, genuinely
  disagreeing (`8.0`, `8.25`, `8.5`, `8.75`, `9.0`, and more).
- Pearson versus Spearman: a perfect parabola scoring essentially zero on
  Pearson while a perfect monotone cubic scores exactly 1.0 on Spearman.
- Anscombe's quartet, verified and then separated by leverage, outlier
  ratio, and residual sign changes.
- Simpson's paradox: the smallest table where a treatment wins every
  subgroup and loses overall, both directions verified arithmetically.
- Robust spread under contamination: a measured 15.10x inflation in the
  standard deviation against a 1.00x change in the median absolute
  deviation, from 3% contaminated data.
- Standardisation: mean 0, standard deviation 1 by construction, and a
  Pearson correlation unchanged to floating-point noise (3.33e-16).

## How this lesson is rendered

`index.mdx` provides the lesson body, and the sidecar YAML files travel
with it: the site layout renders the body at the day route, injects the
quiz from `quiz.yml`, the glossary from `glossary.yml`, and the source
list from `sources.yml`, and uses `lesson.yml` for navigation, metadata
and time estimates. Images are referenced from `index.mdx` with relative
paths into `assets/`, and their alt text stays identical to `visuals.yml`.
All cross-links — to the lab and to neighbouring days — are generated by
the layout from central configuration; never hard-code repository or site
URLs inside content files.

`same-summary-different-data-flow.svg` is animated with CSS `@keyframes`
inside the file, so it animates wherever it is rendered as an image and
prints as a still. It carries a `@media (prefers-reduced-motion: reduce)`
block that disables every animation and restores the resting state, and
the still frame carries the whole meaning on its own — all four scatter
panels, the full statistics panel, and every caption are present without
motion. Both SVGs prefix every class, id and keyframes name — `d116a-` in
the architecture diagram, `d116f-` in the flow diagram — because an
inlined SVG's `<style>` block is not scoped to that SVG and `url(#name)`
resolves document-wide, so two diagrams on one page would otherwise
restyle each other.

No LaTeX appears anywhere in `index.mdx`. Every formula is written in a
fenced ` ```text ` block or inline backticks, because MDX parses `{` and
`}` as JSX and a `$$…$$` block broke the site build on Day 109.
