# Day 100 — Matrices and What They Represent

The second lesson of Week 15 ("Linear Algebra I: Vectors and Matrices"), and
the first day of the course to treat a matrix as an object rather than as a
container. Day 99 introduced vectors; this day introduces the rectangle they
stack into, and Days 101 and 102 build multiplication and linear
transformations on top of it.

The through-line, stated in the first section and returned to in every section
after it: **a matrix is not one thing, and most confusion about matrices comes
from not knowing which of its three meanings is in play.**

1. **A table of data** — rows are items, columns are features. The shape a CSV
   file (Day 65) and a `SELECT` (Day 85) arrive in.
2. **A collection of vectors** — three vectors of length 4 read one way, four
   vectors of length 3 read the other, with entirely different norms.
3. **A transformation** — a machine that eats a vector of one length and
   returns a vector of another. The meaning Day 102 builds on.

The lesson opens by breaking something: a two-line row-centring step that runs
without an exception, returns the shape you expected, and is wrong — because
the matrix was square and a `(4,)` result padded left instead of right. That
bug is the day's spine. It reappears in the broadcasting section, in the
`keepdims` argument, in the quiz, and in the lab, and it is the reason this
day's matrix is deliberately `(3, 4)` rather than square: when the two
dimensions differ, the shape of an answer tells you which operation produced
it.

The rest is the mechanics, each landed against real captured output: shape and
why `(rows, columns)` is worth saying out loud; indexing from 0 against a
paper's from-1 subscripts; rows versus columns and their asymmetric cost;
zero, identity, diagonal and symmetric matrices and what each one *does*;
transpose with a concrete reason to want it; addition and scalar
multiplication; broadcasting taught from the five-line rule with a success, a
failure and a silent trap; reshaping, flattening and slicing with the
view-versus-copy table; and `axis=0` against `axis=1` settled by the rule that
the axis you name is the axis that disappears.

The from-scratch build is a matrix class over nested lists — shape, indexing,
transpose, addition, scalar multiplication — asserted against NumPy, and then
shown failing at the one thing NumPy does effortlessly. That refusal is the
hinge of the lesson: it is what makes broadcasting visible as a feature rather
than as background.

## How this lesson is rendered

`index.mdx` provides the lesson body, and the sidecar YAML files travel with
it: the site layout renders the body at the day route, injects the quiz from
`quiz.yml`, the glossary from `glossary.yml`, and the source list from
`sources.yml`, and uses `lesson.yml` for navigation, metadata and time
estimates. Images are referenced from `index.mdx` with relative paths into
`assets/`, and their alt text must stay identical to `visuals.yml`. All
cross-links — to the lab and to neighbouring days — are generated by the
layout from central configuration; never hard-code repository or site URLs
inside content files.

`broadcasting-flow.svg` is animated with CSS `@keyframes` inside the file, so
it animates wherever it is rendered as an image and prints as a still. It
carries a `@media (prefers-reduced-motion: reduce)` block that disables every
animation and restores the resting state, and the still frame carries the
whole meaning on its own — every number, shape, product and error message is
present without motion. Both SVGs prefix every class, id and keyframes name —
`d100a-` in the architecture diagram, `d100f-` in the flow diagram — because
an inlined SVG's `<style>` block is not scoped to that SVG and `url(#name)`
resolves document-wide, so two diagrams on one page would otherwise restyle
each other.
