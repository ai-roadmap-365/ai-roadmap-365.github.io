# Day 101 — Matrix Multiplication

The third lesson of Week 15 ("Linear Algebra I: Vectors and Matrices"). Day 99
gave the reader vectors; Day 100 gave them matrices and the three ways to read
one. This day is the operation that makes the third reading — a matrix as a
transformation — worth having, and Day 102 builds linear transformations
directly on top of it.

The organising idea, stated in the first section and returned to in every
section after it:

> **Matrix multiplication looks like an arbitrary rule until you see it as
> composition — doing one transformation and then another — and then it is the
> only rule it could be.**

Everything else in the lesson is derived from that rather than asserted
alongside it. The inner dimensions must match because the second machine has to
accept what the first one produces; that inner dimension is then consumed
because the combined machine has no intermediate bench; the outer dimensions
survive because they are the two ends of the pipeline. The reader should finish
able to reconstruct the shape rule from scratch rather than recall it.

The lesson opens by breaking something: `P * Q`, `P @ Q` and `Q @ P` on two 2 by
2 matrices, all three legal, all three returning `(2, 2)`, all three giving
completely different numbers, with no warning of any kind. That trap is the
day's spine and it reappears in the `*`-versus-`@` section, in the quiz, and in
the lab.

The payoff at the end is the forward pass: `X @ W + b`, computed by hand on
numbers small enough to check, followed by the demonstration that two layers
with no activation between them collapse into a single matrix — associativity
doing something the reader can feel.

## The measurement that changed the lesson

The lesson was planned around "the Python loop loses to NumPy because NumPy is
compiled C calling BLAS". The measurement disagreed, and the measurement won.

Timing the same 200 by 200 product three ways showed the Python loop at
0.1957 s, NumPy on `int64` at 0.002479 s, and NumPy on `float64` at 0.000037 s.
**The dtype mattered more than expected: float64 was 66 times faster than int64,
same shapes, same values, same operator.** BLAS implements matrix multiply only
for floating-point and complex types, so an integer product never reaches BLAS
and falls back to NumPy's own compiled loop.

This is now a load-bearing part of the lesson rather than a footnote, because it
makes the usual explanation demonstrably wrong: the `int64` row *is* C, and it is
the slow NumPy row. It is also one of the quiz questions. Do not soften it back
into "NumPy is fast because it is C" — that is the claim the evidence refuted.

## How this lesson is rendered

`index.mdx` provides the lesson body, and the sidecar YAML files travel with it:
the site layout renders the body at the day route, injects the quiz from
`quiz.yml`, the glossary from `glossary.yml`, and the source list from
`sources.yml`, and uses `lesson.yml` for navigation, metadata and time
estimates. Images are referenced from `index.mdx` with relative paths into
`assets/`, and their alt text must stay identical to `visuals.yml`. All
cross-links — to the lab and to neighbouring days — are generated by the layout
from central configuration; never hard-code repository or site URLs inside
content files.

`matmul-composition-flow.svg` is animated with CSS `@keyframes` inside the file,
so it animates wherever it is rendered as an image and prints as a still. It
carries a `@media (prefers-reduced-motion: reduce)` block that disables every
animation and restores the resting state, and the still frame carries the whole
meaning on its own — every coordinate, matrix, arrow and caption is present
without motion, which is what lets the still image *prove* composition rather
than assert it. Both SVGs prefix every class, id and keyframes name —
`d101a-` in the architecture diagram, `d101f-` in the flow diagram — because an
inlined SVG's `<style>` block is not scoped to that SVG and `url(#name)` resolves
document-wide, so two diagrams on one page would otherwise restyle each other.

  specification.
- The historical facts — the Nine Chapters, Sylvester coining "matrix" in 1850,
  Cayley's *A memoir on the theory of matrices* in 1858 and his definition of

  1990 extensions, Strassen in 1969 — were read from the sources listed in
  `sources.yml` at the time of writing. Re-verify before changing any of them.
  Do not add dates that are not in a cited source.
- The row-versus-column convention section is deliberate and must stay. The
  lesson teaches composition with column vectors (rightmost acts first) and then
  writes a layer as `X @ W` with the data on the left. Those look contradictory
  and are not, and a reader who is not told will assume one of the two is a
  mistake. The lab demonstrates that `x @ W` and `W.T @ x` agree.
- The silent integer overflow is verified, not reasoned about: NumPy returns
  `-9223372036709301616` where the true answer is `9223372037000250000`, and
  raises no warning. The lab asserts it including the absence of a warning. Do

  described from their own documentation and explicitly marked as not installed,
