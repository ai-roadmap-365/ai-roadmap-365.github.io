# Day 115 — Bayes' Theorem

The third lesson of Week 17 ("Probability and Statistics"), arriving
directly after Day 113's sample-space foundations and Day 114's random
variables. Week 17 ends in an A/B Test Analyzer on Day 119; today supplies
the reversal of Day 113's law of total probability that the rest of the
week's inferential machinery leans on.

The organising sentence, returned to throughout:

> **Bayes' theorem is the law of total probability read backwards, and
> human intuition fails it so reliably that the failure has a name.**

## The opening failure

The lesson opens with the most reliably misjudged question in applied
probability: a test is 99% sensitive and 99% specific, the condition
affects 1 person in 1,000, you test positive — what's the probability you
actually have it? Almost everyone, including most physicians asked the
same question in published studies, answers "about 99%". The true answer
is close to 9% — exactly `99/1098`, which reduces to `11/122`. The lesson
derives that number exactly, rebuilds it as a natural-frequencies count
over 100,000 people, and confirms it a third way with a two-million-person
seeded simulation — three independent methods landing on the identical
fraction.

## What the lesson covers, each with a worked number

- The two-line derivation from the definition of conditional probability,
  and the four named parts: prior, likelihood, evidence, posterior.
- Why the evidence term (Bayes' theorem's denominator) is exactly Day
  113's law of total probability, applied to the event actually observed.
- The natural-frequencies reframing: the same arithmetic, counted over
  100,000 people instead of multiplied as percentages.
- The odds form — posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio — and why
  it isolates a piece of evidence's worth independently of the prior.
- Sequential updating across two different tests, with both orderings
  computed explicitly and shown identical, because multiplication is
  commutative.
- **The honest caveat most treatments skip:** a concrete correlated-tests
  case (the same assay run twice on one sample) where naively multiplying
  likelihood ratios overstates confidence — `363/400 = 0.9075` naive
  against `2189/13400 ≈ 0.1634` correct.
- Base-rate neglect and the prosecutor's fallacy, each with a worked
  example, and the prevalence sweep showing `0.99` become the genuinely
  correct answer once the base rate reaches 1-in-2.
- A from-scratch Naive Bayes spam classifier confronting the one-word veto
  (unsmoothed zero-probability words) and the log-space underflow (500
  factors of `0.01` collapsing to exactly `0.0` in float64).

## How this lesson is rendered

`index.mdx` provides the lesson body, and the sidecar YAML files travel
with it: the site layout renders the body at the day route, injects the
quiz from `quiz.yml`, the glossary from `glossary.yml`, and the source
list from `sources.yml`, and uses `lesson.yml` for navigation, metadata
and time estimates. Images are referenced from `index.mdx` with relative
paths into `assets/`, and their alt text stays identical to `visuals.yml`.
All cross-links — to the lab and to neighbouring days — are generated by
the layout from central configuration; never hard-code repository or site
URLs inside content files.

`posterior-update-flow.svg` is animated with CSS `@keyframes` inside the
file, so it animates wherever it is rendered as an image and prints as a
still. It carries a `@media (prefers-reduced-motion: reduce)` block that
disables every animation and restores the resting state, and the still
frame carries the whole meaning on its own — both panels, both bar charts,
every label and the full footer text are present without motion. Both
SVGs prefix every class, id and keyframes name — `d115a-` in the
architecture diagram, `d115f-` in the flow diagram — because an inlined
SVG's `<style>` block is not scoped to that SVG and `url(#name)` resolves
document-wide, so two diagrams on one page would otherwise restyle each
other.

No LaTeX appears anywhere in `index.mdx`. Every formula is written in a
fenced ` ```text ` block or inline backticks, because MDX parses `{` and
`}` as JSX and a `$$…$$` block broke the site build on Day 109.
